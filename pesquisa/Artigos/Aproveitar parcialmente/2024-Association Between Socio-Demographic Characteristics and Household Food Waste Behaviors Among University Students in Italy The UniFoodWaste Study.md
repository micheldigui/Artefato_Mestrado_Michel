Abstract: Food waste is a growing environmental, economic, and social challenge worldwide.
This study aimed to assess the association between various socio-demographic characteristics and
household food waste behaviors among university students in Italy. A cross-sectional study was
conducted using a validated, online, anonymous, and voluntary-based questionnaire distributed
among university students in Milan, Italy. Linear regression analyses were performed to explore the
association between socio-demographic factors and anti-household food waste behaviors (preventive
practice, abilities scores, and parental guidance). A total of 2779 students participated in the survey.
Students residing outside Lombardy exhibited higher preventive practice scores (β = 0.11, p = 0.030).
Regular smoking was negatively associated with preventive practice (β = −0.22, p < 0.001) and
abilities scores (β = −0.11, p = 0.009). Cohabitation with a partner positively impacted abilities scores
(β = 0.23, p = 0.007). Larger family sizes and students in scientific fields showed higher scores for
parents’ guidance (β = 0.18, p < 0.001). Frequent use of food delivery apps correlated with lower
scores across all categories, while anti-food waste apps had a positive effect. Socio-demographic

While significant quantities of food are wasted, food insecurity and malnutrition remain
pressing challenges in many parts of the world, including within developed countries like
Italy. This dichotomy highlights a significant social injustice, where surplus food coexists
with widespread hunger.

In recent years, attention has increasingly focused on understanding and mitigating
food waste at the household level, recognizing its contribution to the overall waste stream.
Household food waste specifically refers to food discarded in the domestic setting, whether
during meal preparation or from uneaten leftovers [10]. This type of waste is particularly
concerning because it represents the final link in the food supply chain, where the preven-
tion of waste can have immediate environmental, economic, and social benefits [11]. Unlike
food loss, which occurs at earlier stages of the supply chain due to reasons such as poor
infrastructure or logistics, household food waste is often the result of consumer behaviors,
including over-purchasing, poor planning, and misunderstanding of food labels [12].

A growing body of research has explored food waste behaviors within households and
the general population, but there is a notable gap in understanding the factors that influence
food waste among specific demographic groups, such as university students. Studies in
various countries have indicated that college students may exhibit distinct food waste
patterns compared to the general population, largely due to their unique living conditions,
financial constraints, and limited cooking experience [13]. University students often live
independently for the first time, making decisions about food purchasing and preparation
without the guidance they might have received at home. These unique living conditions
and lifestyle choices can significantly impact their food waste patterns. Research conducted
in the United States and the United Kingdom suggests that factors such as irregular eating
habits, lack of food storage knowledge, and time constraints contribute significantly to
food waste among students [14]. Moreover, studies have shown that students often face
challenges in food management, including planning and budgeting, which can lead to the
disposal of edible food [15].

While several studies have explored food waste in households and among the gen-
eral population, there remains a significant gap in understanding the specific behaviors
and influences among university students in Italy. Given the significant role that socio-
demographic characteristics—such as age, gender, and living arrangements—play in shap-
ing students’ food-related behaviors, it is essential to explore how these factors influence
food waste among this group. Additionally, the use of digital tools, such as food-related
apps, may further affect students’ food purchasing and consumption habits, thus impacting
food waste generation.

This study aims to bridge this gap by assessing how socio-demographic characteristics
such as age, sex, residency, educational level, and use of food apps, among others, relate to
household food waste behavior in this population. Such insights are critical for developing
tailored interventions that can effectively reduce food waste among university students. The
primary objective of this study is to measure household food waste behavior, and secondly,
to assess the association between socio-demographic characteristics and household food
waste behavior among university students in Italy. By identifying key factors influencing
waste behavior, this research seeks to contribute to the development of more effective public
health policies and educational programs aimed at reducing food waste. This, in turn, can
lead to significant improvements in environmental sustainability, economic efficiency, and
social equity.

2. Materials and Methods
2.1. Study Design

This research employed a cross-sectional study design to explore the relationship
between various socio-demographic characteristics and household food waste behaviors
among university students in Italy. Data were collected between July and October 2023,
allowing for a comprehensive snapshot of the study period. The cross-sectional approach
enabled the researchers to analyze data from a large number of participants at a single point



Sustainability 2024, 16, 9279 3 of 14

in time, thereby facilitating the identification of patterns and correlations. More details
have been previously published [16], providing additional context and validation for the
research design.

2.2. Participants
Eligibility for participation in the study was determined based on the following criteria:

being a registered university student at University of Milan, aged 18 years or older, residing
in Italy during the study period, and willing to provide informed consent. Participation
was entirely voluntary, with no incentives provided for completion or participation. This
ensured that the data collected reflected genuine responses without any external influences.
The participant pool was intended to represent the broader demographic diversity of
university students, capturing a range of ages, backgrounds, and living situations.

2.3. Data Collection
Data were anonymously collected using a self-administered, online questionnaire

distributed through university email lists. The questionnaire included sections on socio-
demographic characteristics, food waste behavior, and the use of food apps for delivery
and waste prevention. The questionnaire was implemented using Microsoft Forms (Mi-
crosoft 365 A5) and distributed via the university’s official mailing list to ensure that only
University of Milan students could participate. To guarantee that only University of Milan
students could participate, access to the questionnaire was restricted to university users,
and measures were taken to prevent duplicate submissions. Additionally, to tackle the
problem of incomplete responses or missing data, all questions were set as mandatory.
This requirement prevented users from moving to other sections of the questionnaire until
they had answered all questions, thereby improving the completeness and reliability of
the collected data. Before filling out the questionnaire, participants were asked to review
the study’s objectives, along with guidance on how to participate. Additionally, each
participant was required to give informed consent, and those who chose not to consent
were directed to the end of the questionnaire.

2.4. Socio-Demographic Data
The initial section of the survey delved into socio-demographic aspects such as gen-

der (options: female, male, prefer not to say), age, region of residency, smoking habit
(yes/no), level of education (high school, degree or higher), cohabitation (alone, with fam-
ily, with a partner, with friends/roommates), family members, student status (commuter,
off-site, living in Milan), study area (health sciences, humanities, science and technology,
social sciences), and the use of apps related to food (including delivery and saving food).
This comprehensive demographic profiling allowed for a nuanced understanding of the
participants’ backgrounds and potential influences on their food waste behaviors.

2.5. Household Food Waste
The household food waste behaviors (HFWB) questionnaire was used to gather data on

food waste behaviors, including precautionary measures, skills related to food management,
and parental guidance on waste prevention [17]. In detail, the HFWB questionnaire is a
validated and widely utilized tool in research, featuring 10 questions on a Likert scale that
span from 0 (indicating never or strong disagreement) to 6 (signifying always or strong
agreement). In the study, its objective was to gauge consumer practices concerning food
wastage. Specifically, the questionnaire aimed to measure (i) preventive practices (such as
strategizing for grocery shopping and food utilization, eschewing impulsive buys, keeping
track of food inventory, preparing meals in suitable amounts, and repurposing leftovers),
(ii) abilities (such as perceived difficulties in determining food freshness, inventive cooking,
precise planning, and knowledge about prolonging food life), and (iii) the influence of
parental guidance on minimizing food waste (highlighting parental stress on reducing
food wastage). An overall total food waste score was calculated as the mean between the

scores of the three sections (preventive practices, abilities, influence of parental guidance on
minimizing food waste). The final score ranged between 0 and 6, providing a quantifiable
measure of household food waste behavior.

2.6. Statistical Analysis
Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the socio-demographic characteristics of

the participants and their food waste behaviors, reporting the mean and standard deviation
(SD) for continuous variables and the frequency and percentages for categorical ones.
Multicollinearity between each predictor was assessed using the Pearson’s correlation test
(|rho| ≥ 0.7). We calculated the score of each component as the mean of the items of each
component and the overall waste score as the mean of the items of the HFWB questionnaire.
Due to the continuous nature of each score, we decided to perform four linear regression
models (three models for the three component scores and one model for the overall waste
score) to assess the association between each socio-demographic factor and anti-food waste
behavior, adjusting for age and sex. All the scores were standardized before performing
linear regression models. The beta coefficients (β) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were
calculated to estimate the strength and direction of these associations. A p-value of less
than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed
using R version 4.3.3, ensuring robust and reproducible results.

2.7. Sample Size Calculation
The calculation of the sample size was performed using a confidence level of 95%

and a margin of error of 5%. The reference population for the study was the total student
population enrolled at the University of Milan during the 2021/2022 academic year, which
consisted of 60,988 students (53). Since the proportion of the Italian student population
exhibiting food waste behaviors is not known, we adopted a conservative approach, assum-
ing a proportion value of 50%. Based on these parameters, the minimum required sample
size was calculated to be 382 participants.

2.8. Ethical Approval
This study received ethical approval from the Ethical Committee of the University of

Milan, ID n. 71.23. This approval underscores the study’s adherence to ethical standards
in research, ensuring that participants’ rights and welfare were protected throughout
the study.

3. Results
3.1. Participant Demographics

The study initially surveyed 2779 university students; however, 82 students withdrew
consent for data processing and were therefore excluded, resulting in a final sample size
of 2697 subjects. Participants were nearly equally distributed among the age groups, with
52.4% (n = 1414) aged between 18 and 23 years, and 47.6% (n = 1283) aged 24 years or
older. The majority of respondents were female (70.5%, n = 1902), non-smokers (71.4%,
n = 1927), held high school diplomas (52.5%, n = 1417), lived with their own families
(68.4%, n = 1845), and were commuters (51.8%, n = 1398). Academically, the students were
distributed across several fields, with 35.0% (n = 944) studying Science and Technology,
followed by 24.1% (n = 649) in Humanities, 22.8% (n = 616) in Health Sciences, and 18.1%
(n = 488) in Social Sciences.

Regarding food apps usage, 57.4% (n = 1547) reported not using food delivery apps,
38.9% (n = 1050) used them rarely, and only 3.7% (n = 100) used them at least once a
week. Similarly, most students did not use food waste apps (63.5%, n = 1712), while 33.9%
(n = 914) used them infrequently, and a small fraction (2.6%, n = 71) reported weekly usage.

The students’ self-reported scores on prevention practices averaged 5.51 (SD = 0.75),
while abilities scored 5.03 (SD = 0.97). Education from parents received a higher rating,
with an average score of 5.91 (SD = 1.18), and the overall food waste management score

personal habits and a moderate engagement with health and technology among the student

4. Discussion
4.1. Interpretation of Results

Our study identified several socio-demographic characteristics that significantly influ-
ence household food waste behaviors among university students in Italy. These findings
provide a comprehensive understanding of how different factors shape food waste practices
within this population. However, it is essential to consider the representativeness of our
sample when interpreting these results. While the sample includes students from various
regions and academic disciplines, the inherent diversity does not necessarily imply that the
findings are generalizable to all university students or young adults across Italy or beyond.

The observation that students living outside Lombardy demonstrated slightly higher
scores in preventive practices compared to their counterparts in other areas might suggest
regional variations in lifestyle or food-related culture. This aligns with research indicat-
ing that human behavior and lifestyles can be significantly influenced by differences in
community norms, available resources, and local policies [18]. Regional differences in food
availability, culinary traditions, and public awareness campaigns might contribute to these
variations in food waste behaviors. For instance, Lombardy’s more urbanized setting might
offer different food access and storage opportunities than rural areas, leading to distinct
waste behaviors. However, the sample’s composition, with a relatively higher proportion
of participants from specific regions, limits our ability to fully extrapolate these findings to
other areas of Italy.

Notably, regular smoking and frequent use of food delivery apps were associated
with a reduced likelihood of engaging in food waste-preventive behaviors. This finding
suggests a potential avenue for targeted interventions that address lifestyle choices as
part of broader food waste reduction strategies. Frequent use of food delivery apps may
indicate a more “convenience”-oriented lifestyle, which could correlate with less planning
and less mindfulness about food consumption and waste. Convenience-driven behaviors,
such as ordering takeout, often result in less engagement with meal preparation and food
management, potentially leading to increased food waste due to over-ordering or improper
storage of leftovers [19]. This pattern of behavior aligns with findings observed during
the COVID-19 lockdowns, where convenience and dietary habits, including adherence to
the Mediterranean Diet, were significantly impacted [20]. Conversely, larger household
sizes and certain academic disciplines (specifically, scientific and technological fields) were
linked to a greater propensity for adopting anti-waste behaviors, highlighting the role of
community and educational backgrounds in shaping attitudes towards food waste.

The negative correlation between smoking habits and food waste-preventive be-
haviors requires further exploration. One possible explanation is that smoking, as a
health-compromising behavior, may be indicative of lower overall engagement in health-
promoting or environmentally sustainable practices. Smokers may be less likely to plan
meals or consider the environmental impact of their food choices, resulting in higher food
waste [21].

Moreover, previous studies have suggested a link between broader lifestyle patterns
and environmental behaviors [22,23]. For instance, research has shown that individuals
engaged in health-compromising behaviors, such as smoking, may be less likely to partici-
pate in environmentally sustainable actions, such as waste prevention [13], possibly due to
differing priorities or levels of awareness [24].

The positive impact of household size on food waste-preventive behaviors corrob-
orates findings from earlier research, which posits that larger families are more efficient
in food management, possibly due to the economies of scale in food preparation and the
shared responsibility over food resources [25–27]. Among the often-neglected features
of healthy diets, such as the Mediterranean Diet, is the preparation and sharing of food.

Furthermore, living with family was associated with stronger parental guidance against
food waste, emphasizing the role of family dynamics in fostering environmental steward-
ship [28]. In the context of the Mediterranean Diet, conviviality and commensality play
crucial roles. The preparation and sharing of food within a family setting enhances the
efficiency of food management, as family members are more likely to share meals and
collaborate in meal planning and preparation. This collective approach not only reduces
food waste but also promotes healthier eating habits and strengthens family bonds [29].

Moreover, while our sample included students from diverse academic disciplines,
the overrepresentation of students in scientific and technological fields, who displayed a
greater propensity for adopting anti-waste behaviors, might have introduced bias. This
group’s heightened awareness of environmental sustainability through their curricula could
disproportionately influence the study’s overall results. Although this finding supports
the hypothesis that education plays a role in shaping food waste behaviors, it also raises
questions about the extent to which this influence applies to students in non-scientific
fields [23]. In this perspective, educational programs, therefore, have the potential to tailor
their content to enhance not only academic skills but also personal and environmental
responsibility. Future research could balance the sample across disciplines to better assess
the generalizability of these findings.

Lastly, the divergent effects of app usage on household food waste behaviors are
particularly notable. While food delivery apps were associated with pro-food waste behav-
ior [30], the positive impact of anti-food waste apps highlights the potential of technology
to support environmental and health goals. This duality underscores the importance of
discerning the type of technology used and its potential impacts on behavior.

The literature agrees that household food waste has its roots in human wasteful
behaviors [31,32]. Behaviors emerge as the result of how psychological factors interact with
the context in which people live [33]. In light of this, three main theories in the literature try
to explain how wasteful behaviors are generated. The Theory of Planned Behaviors focuses
on psychological aspects: attitude, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control
are the three determinants of intention that, in turn, directly determine behaviors [34]. In
this theory, perceived behavioral control also constitutes a direct determinant of behaviors.
This means that the stronger the intentions and/or perceived behavioral control to avoid
wasteful behaviors, the higher the probability of avoiding those behaviors [10,35].

The Norm Activation Model is another theory based on psychological factors that
has two main interpretations [36]. Briefly, in this model personal norms, seen as feelings
of moral obligation, are directly linked to behaviors. In this relationship, awareness of
the consequences of these behaviors and ascribed responsibility in performing or not
performing them can act both as moderators (moderator model) or as determinants of
personal norms (mediator model) [37]. Applying this theory to the topic of food waste
emphasizes how personal norms are active and can prevent wasteful behaviors when
consumers are aware and feel responsible for the negative impacts of food waste [38,39].

Finally, the third construct is the Social Practice Theory that shifts the focus from
individuals to the society [40]. In this model, conventions, institutions, infrastructures,
and access to resources, or in other terms, the environment, play a fundamental role in
determining one’s behavior. Practice is viewed as the result of interconnected elements:
materials, meaning, and competence. In this perspective, food waste behaviors can be
prevented by making available the technologies to reduce food waste, by making those
activities that prevent food waste meaningful for individuals and well established in their
routines, and finally, by promoting knowledge on practices and reasons to reduce food
waste [41–43].

This last model offers a more holistic approach to understanding the deep causes
of wasteful behaviors and tends to reflect the complexity of a phenomenon that is multi-
faceted [44]. Some studies have already investigated the drivers of and barriers to food
waste reduction at the household level [45,46]. Including socio-demographic characteristics
in these evaluations is essential. In fact, they can help in giving a shape to that environment

that according to the Social Practice Theory is crucial in influencing food waste behaviors.
In this light, belonging to older age groups, having a higher educational level, and having
a lower income were found to be less likely associated with food waste behaviors [3,47,48].
However, evidence seems controversial and profoundly linked to the setting in which these
studies are performed [49].

In summary, our findings reveal that lifestyle choices, such as smoking and the use
of food delivery apps, are significantly associated with a lower likelihood of engaging in
behaviors that prevent food waste. In contrast, factors such as living in larger households,
studying in scientific or technological fields, and the occasional use of food-saving apps
correlate with a higher propensity for adopting anti-waste behaviors.

4.2. Implications for Public Health Policies
These findings underscore the importance of incorporating socio-demographic in-

sights into the design of public health policies aimed at reducing food waste. Tailored
interventions that consider individual lifestyle factors and leverage educational settings
could enhance the effectiveness of food waste reduction campaigns. For instance, initiatives
that engage students in scientific and technological fields could harness their propensity
for anti-waste behaviors, turning them into advocates for food sustainability within their
communities. Educational institutions can integrate food waste reduction topics into their
curricula and promote hands-on projects that encourage students to apply their knowledge
in real-world settings. Regional differences in food waste behaviors suggest that localized
policies and interventions may be more effective than a one-size-fits-all approach. For
instance, students living outside Lombardy displayed better preventive practices, indicat-
ing that regional cultural norms and policies may influence food waste behaviors. Local
governments could develop region-specific strategies that align with the unique cultural
and economic contexts of their communities.

The negative association between the use of food delivery apps and food waste-
preventive behaviors calls for regulatory measures and educational campaigns to promote
more sustainable food consumption practices among users [50]. Public health policies
could encourage the incorporation of eco-friendly options within these apps, such as
reminders to use leftovers or discounts for ordering smaller portions [51]. Moreover,
collaborations with food delivery services to highlight sustainable choices could enhance
consumer awareness and reduce food waste [52]. Furthermore, this study highlights the
potential of using technology, such as food-saving apps, as a tool for promoting food waste-
preventive behaviors. Public policies could support the development and dissemination
of such technologies, making them more accessible and appealing to the general public,
including university students. By leveraging the growing popularity of apps and digital
platforms, policymakers can create innovative solutions that resonate with tech-savvy
younger generations.

In conclusion, integrating socio-demographic insights into public health policies and
educational programs can significantly enhance efforts to reduce food waste. By addressing
the specific needs and behaviors of different population groups, particularly university
students, targeted interventions can foster more sustainable food consumption practices
and contribute to broader environmental and economic goals.

4.3. Implications for Research
Future research should explore the underlying mechanisms that link socio-demographic

characteristics to food waste behaviors, providing deeper insights into how individual
and collective factors interact to influence waste practices. Understanding these mech-
anisms can help identify key factors that contribute to food waste and inform targeted
interventions. Moreover, future research should explore these relationships in more diverse
populations to understand the generalizability of these findings. Investigating different
cultural, economic, and geographical contexts will provide a broader understanding of
food waste behaviors and their determinants.



Sustainability 2024, 16, 9279 10 of 14

Longitudinal studies could offer a broader understanding of how these behaviors
evolve over time, particularly as students’ transition from university life to full-time
employment. By tracking changes in food waste behaviors, researchers can identify patterns
and influences that persist or change over time. Furthermore, since longitudinal studies
track changes in food waste behaviors over time, they could help determine the long-term
impact of various interventions, such as the introduction of anti-food waste technologies.
Such studies would provide valuable data on the sustainability and effectiveness of these
interventions, enabling continuous improvement of public health policies [11]. There is
also a need to investigate the role of educational programs in shaping food waste behaviors,
particularly among students in non-scientific fields. Expanding environmental education
across all academic disciplines could foster a broader culture of sustainability [53,54]. Future
research could evaluate the impact of integrating food waste education into curricula and
assess the long-term benefits of students’ behaviors [55]. Lastly, examining the role of family
dynamics and household composition in food waste behaviors could provide insights
into how family-oriented interventions might be designed. Understanding how family
interactions and responsibilities influence food waste can help in developing targeted
strategies that address the specific needs of different household types.

In conclusion, addressing food waste requires a comprehensive approach that con-
siders lifestyle choices, technological interventions, educational influences, and family
dynamics. By tailoring public health policies to these various factors, it is possible to foster
more sustainable food consumption behaviors and contribute to broader environmental
and health goals.

4.4. Limitations and Strengths
This study is not without limitations, including its cross-sectional design, which

precludes causal inferences, and the self-reported nature of the data, which may be subject
to reporting bias or social desirability bias. Future studies could employ more objective
measures of food waste and explore causal relationships through experimental designs.
Lastly, despite our sample being characterized by a wide range of students from different
Italian regions, the representativeness of our study is not nationwide, as universities from
various regions of Italy were not included. In this context, future studies could benefit
from a multi-center design, which would allow for a more stratified sampling approach
that could ensure proportional representation from all regions. Nevertheless, the current
study also has important strengths. First, the large sample size of university students is
a major strength of the study. By encompassing a wide range of ages, educational levels,
disciplinary fields, and living situations, the study provides comprehensive insights into
the food waste behaviors of young adults in various socio-demographic contexts. This
diversity enhances the generalizability of the findings and ensures that the conclusions
drawn are relevant to a broad segment of the university student population. Second,
the use of a validated questionnaire to assess household food waste behavior represents
another strength of the study. By including items that capture precautionary measures,
food management skills, and the influence of parental guidance, the questionnaire offers
a detailed and nuanced understanding of behaviors and attitudes related to food waste.
This comprehensive approach allows for a more in-depth analysis of the factors influencing
food waste beyond what is typically captured in studies focusing on a narrower set of
behaviors. Lastly, the application of regression analyses to assess the association between
socio-demographic factors and food waste behavior adds statistical rigor to the study. This
methodological approach ensures that the findings are robust and reliable, providing a
solid foundation for future research and policy development.

5. Conclusions
These findings indicate significant associations between certain socio-demographic

characteristics and food waste behaviors among university students in Italy. The observed
relationships suggest that both individual lifestyle choices, such as smoking and the use of



Sustainability 2024, 16, 9279 11 of 14

food delivery apps, and broader socio-demographic factors, such as household size and
field of study, can influence food waste behaviors.

For instance, individual lifestyle choices such as smoking and the use of food delivery
apps have been identified as influencing food waste behaviors. Smokers might exhibit
different food consumption and waste patterns compared to non-smokers, possibly due to
different health awareness levels or time management strategies. Similarly, the increasing
reliance on food delivery apps can affect how students plan their meals, manage leftovers,
and ultimately, how much food they waste. This reliance might lead to over-ordering or less
engagement with traditional meal preparation, resulting in higher amounts of food waste.

Broader socio-demographic factors, such as household size and field of study, also play
a crucial role. Students from larger households may have different food waste behaviors
compared to those from smaller households, potentially due to shared responsibilities
and collective meal planning practices. In larger households, the coordination required to
manage food resources might reduce waste, whereas smaller households might experience
more individualistic consumption patterns, leading to higher waste.

The field of study is another significant factor. Students in scientific fields may have
more exposure to environmental and sustainability issues through their coursework, which
can positively influence their food waste behaviors. Conversely, students in non-scientific
fields might lack this exposure, leading to less awareness and higher food waste. This
highlights the importance of integrating environmental education across all academic disci-
plines to promote sustainable behaviors universally. These relationships underscore the
complexity of food waste behaviors and the need for multifaceted approaches to address
them. By understanding how individual and collective factors interact, targeted interven-
tions can be developed. For example, educational programs tailored to different fields of
study or campaigns that address the specific needs and habits of smokers and food delivery
app users could be more effective in reducing food waste. In summary, the significant
associations between socio-demographic characteristics and food waste behaviors among
university students in Italy highlight the interplay between individual lifestyle choices and
broader socio-demographic factors. These insights can inform the development of targeted
interventions and policies aimed at reducing food waste, ultimately contributing to more
sustainable consumption patterns and environmental preservation.
